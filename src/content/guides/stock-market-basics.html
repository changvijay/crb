What is the stock market?

The stock market is a marketplace where buyers and sellers trade ownership in companies. In India, most trading happens on the NSE and BSE. When you buy a share, you own a small part of that business, and its price moves with the company's performance and market demand.

But shares are only one choice. Money can also be lent, held as property, or placed in gold. Knowing the full map helps you pick what fits your goal, time frame, and comfort with risk.

Key terms every beginner should know

NSE (National Stock Exchange)
India's largest exchange by trading volume. Its main index is the Nifty 50, which tracks 50 large companies.
BSE (Bombay Stock Exchange)
Asia's oldest stock exchange, started in 1875. Its main index is the Sensex, which tracks 30 large companies.
SEBI
The Securities and Exchange Board of India, the regulator that makes the rules for exchanges, brokers, and companies, and protects investors.
Sensex and Nifty
Indices that act as a scorecard for the market. When people say the market is up, they usually mean one of these has risen.
IPO (Initial Public Offering)
The first time a company sells its shares to the public to raise money. Investors apply during a set window, and shares are allotted if the application succeeds. Allotment is not guaranteed when the issue is oversubscribed.
Primary and secondary market
The primary market is where new shares are issued, such as in an IPO. The secondary market is where those shares are then bought and sold between investors on NSE and BSE.
Grey market and GMP
An unofficial, unregulated market where IPO shares are traded informally before they list. The Grey Market Premium (GMP) is the extra price people are willing to pay over the issue price. It hints at expected listing gains, but it is not guaranteed and not a reliable signal on its own.
Demat and trading account
A demat account holds your shares in electronic form. A trading account is used to place buy and sell orders. You need both to invest through a broker.
Bull and bear market
A bull market is a period of rising prices and optimism. A bear market is a period of falling prices and fear.
Market capitalisation
Share price multiplied by the number of shares. It sorts companies into large-cap, mid-cap, and small-cap, with small-caps generally being riskier.
Dividend
A share of company profit paid out to shareholders. Not every company pays one.
Circuit limits
Daily price limits that stop a stock from rising or falling beyond a set percentage in one day, to control extreme swings.

7 types of investments

1. Equity

You own a part of a business
  • Stocks/shares: direct ownership in listed companies.
  • Equity mutual funds and ETFs: a basket of stocks managed by a fund house or tracking an index.
  • Private equity and venture capital: investing in unlisted or early-stage companies, usually for larger amounts and longer lock-ins.

2. Debt

You lend money and earn interest
  • Bonds and debentures: loans to governments or companies that pay regular interest.
  • Fixed deposits (FDs): a fixed rate for a fixed term with a bank.
  • Debt mutual funds and Treasury Bills: pooled or short-term government lending with lower volatility.

3. Hybrid

A mix of equity and debt features
  • Hybrid mutual funds: one fund holding both stocks and bonds.
  • Convertible bonds: bonds that can turn into shares later.
  • REITs and InvITs: trusts that own real estate or infrastructure and pass income to unit holders.

4. Real assets

Physical things with lasting value
  • Real estate: property bought for rent or appreciation.
  • Gold: a traditional hedge against inflation and uncertainty.
  • Commodities: raw materials such as silver, crude oil, and agricultural goods.

5. Alternative investments

Higher risk, outside the usual options
  • Cryptocurrency: digital assets with very sharp price swings.
  • Derivatives (F&O): contracts whose value depends on an underlying asset; losses can exceed expectations.
  • P2P lending and collectibles (NFTs): lending to individuals online, or owning unique digital items.

6. Retirement and tax saving

Built for long-term goals and tax benefits
  • PPF, EPF, and NPS: government-backed schemes for retirement savings.
  • ELSS: equity mutual funds with a tax-saving lock-in period.

7. Cash and cash equivalents

Safe, easy to access, low returns
  • Savings accounts: instant access, modest interest.
  • Money market and liquid funds: short-term parking for money you may need soon.
Your emergency money belongs in the last group. Money you will not need for years can go into the growth groups.

6 trading styles

Trading is about how long you hold and how you decide. The longer the holding period, the more weight fundamentals carry. The shorter it is, the more charts matter, and the higher the risk.

StyleDurationAnalysisRisk
Long term1 year+FundamentalLow
Positional1 month–6 monthsTechnical + fundamentalModerate
Swing1 week–1 monthTechnicalModerate
Short termDay–weekTechnicalModerate–high
Day tradingIntradayTechnicalHigh
ScalpingSeconds–minutesMicro technicalVery high

Fundamental analysis studies a company's earnings, debt, and growth. Technical analysis studies price charts and volume to time entries and exits.

Where to start

Pick your goal first, then your instrument. Beginners usually do well starting with index funds or long-term equity, keeping an emergency fund in cash equivalents, and learning a faster trading style only with money they can afford to lose.

Start small, track your results, and keep learning before you increase risk.
